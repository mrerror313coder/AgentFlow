Retrieval-augmented generation, usually called RAG, attaches a search step to a language model. The model only sees the passages a retriever judged relevant, which cuts the chance that it will invent a citation or a policy that is not in the source. Retrieval matters because a model’s parameters are a lossy memory of training data. They are stale, and they mix sources. A search over documents you control is easier to update than a retraining run.

Keyword search scores overlapping words. It is simple, inspectable, and strong when the query uses the same terms as the notes. Embedding search maps text into vectors and finds neighbors by meaning, which helps when the wording differs. Chunking decides what the retriever can return. Chunks of a few hundred words with a small overlap keep a definition and its example together without dragging in a whole chapter. Too-small chunks lose context. Too-large chunks dilute the match and waste the model’s window. Hallucination drops when the prompt tells the model to answer only from the retrieved text and to say when the notes do not contain the answer. A good RAG system also shows the source file and the score so a person can check the passage instead of trusting the paragraph blindly.
