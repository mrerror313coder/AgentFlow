Large language models are neural networks trained to predict the next token in a sequence of text. A token is a chunk of a word, a whole word, or a piece of punctuation. Modern models use the transformer architecture, whose attention layers let each token look at other tokens in the context window and decide which ones matter. Training runs over enormous collections of internet text, books, and code. The model adjusts billions of parameters so that likely continuations become more probable. After pretraining, instruction tuning and preference data teach the model to answer questions rather than merely continue a paragraph.

Familiar examples include GPT, Llama, and Mistral. GPT models popularized the chat interface. Llama is a family of open-weight models that developers can download and run on their own hardware. Mistral showed that a smaller, carefully trained model can compete with much larger systems on many tasks. These models write, summarize, translate, and draft code, but they do not look up facts unless a surrounding system retrieves them. That limitation is why agent frameworks pair a model with tools, memory, and checks. Running an open-weight model locally keeps prompts on the machine that owns the data and removes a per-token bill, which is the practical reason teams adopt Llama or Mistral for private workflows.
